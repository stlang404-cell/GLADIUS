You probably bought this book after a run of games that went the same way. You played a reasonable opening, you had a decent position, and then a piece disappeared. Maybe you missed a knight fork. Maybe you took a pawn and walked into checkmate. You lost, you started another game straight away, and the same thing happened again.

This book exists to break that cycle. It is a complete training system for going from about 400 to about 1500 Elo. It does not hand you a pile of facts to memorize. It teaches you what to look at, what to decide, and what to do at the board, and it gives you a routine that turns each game into improvement instead of just another result.

## Who this book is for

This book is written for an adult or teenage player who:

* knows how the pieces move, or mostly knows (Chapter 1 fills every gap);
* plays online or over the board and is rated somewhere around 400 to 1000, or has no rating yet;
* wants a structured plan rather than random videos and puzzles;
* is willing to do some real work: solving positions with a pencil, playing slower games, and looking honestly at their own mistakes.

You do not need a coach, a club, or a paid subscription. A physical chess set or any free chess app is enough. If you are already above 1000, you can still use the book: skim the early chapters, take the stage tests, and start where the tests show gaps.

## The core loop: LEARN, SOLVE, PLAY, ANALYZE, FIX, REPEAT

Every chapter plugs into the same six-step loop. The loop matters more than any single chapter, because reading alone does not change how you play. Here is what each step means in practice.

**LEARN.** Read the chapter with a board in front of you. Set up every diagram and play through every move sequence. When the text says "First I check...", stop and do the check yourself before reading on. Plan on 45 to 90 minutes per chapter, split over two or three sittings if you like. A chapter you read on your phone while waiting for a bus has not been learned.

**SOLVE.** Each chapter ends with 11 exercises. Solve them as described below: honestly, without moving pieces, writing your answer down before you look. The exercises are where the chapter's idea moves from "I understood it" to "I can find it".

**PLAY.** Play at least two or three slow games that week. Each chapter ends with a concrete assignment for your next game, such as "before every move, check all of your opponent's checks and captures". Try to carry out that one instruction in every game, even if you forget everything else.

**ANALYZE.** After each game, before you switch on an engine, go through the game on your own for ten minutes. Find the move where the game turned. Write down what you were thinking at that moment, if you can remember. Only then check the game with an engine, and compare what it says with what you thought.

**FIX.** Turn each serious mistake into a one-line entry in a mistake journal: the date, the position (a FEN or a photo is fine), what you played, what was better, and a category such as *hanging piece*, *missed tactic*, *king safety* or *endgame*. After ten games, count the categories. The biggest category tells you which chapter to go back to. Chapter 28 shows the full method.

**REPEAT.** Go round the loop again with the next chapter, and keep revisiting the mistakes you have already logged. Most improvement at this level comes from fixing the same two or three habits many times over, not from learning something new every day.

:::key The loop in one sentence
Learn an idea, drill it until you can find it, use it in real games, look honestly at what happened, and fix the pattern that cost you points.
:::

## The five stages

The book is divided into five stages. The rating ranges are milestones that describe typical needs. They are not entry requirements and they are not promises. People improve at very different speeds, and ratings on different sites are not directly comparable.

| Stage | Rating range | Main goal | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 400–600 | Eliminate elementary blunders | 1–6 |
| 2 | 600–800 | Tactical awareness | 7–13 |
| 3 | 800–1000 | Calculation and conversion | 14–18 |
| 4 | 1000–1200 | Positional judgment | 19–23 |
| 5 | 1200–1500 | Calculation, planning and endgame technique | 24–28 |

**Stage 1 (400–600): eliminate elementary blunders.** Most games at this level are decided by a piece left hanging or a one-move checkmate. You will learn the complete rules, read and write notation, count attackers and defenders, run a safety scan before every move, deliver the basic checkmates, and play sound opening moves.

**Stage 2 (600–800): tactical awareness.** Now the games are decided by two-move ideas: forks, pins, skewers, discovered attacks and simple mating patterns. You will also build a small, reliable opening repertoire for White and Black.

**Stage 3 (800–1000): calculation and conversion.** You will learn a move-selection routine, calculate short forcing lines accurately, turn winning positions into wins, defend worse positions stubbornly, and play basic king and pawn endgames.

**Stage 4 (1000–1200): positional judgment.** Here you learn what to do when there is no tactic: improving your worst piece, understanding pawn structures, attacking a weakened king, making a plan, and handling the most common rook endgames.

**Stage 5 (1200–1500): calculation, planning and endgame technique.** Harder tactics, longer calculation, middlegame strategy, technical endgames, practical decision-making under the clock, and a lifelong method for analyzing your own games.

Each stage ends with a test of 15 positions. Use the tests as checkpoints. If you are already strong in a stage, the test will show it, and you can move on. If you struggle, the test will point you to the chapters to revisit.

:::coach Do not skip Stage 1
Coaching judgment: many players who feel "beyond the basics" still lose a large share of their games to pieces left undefended. Even if you are rated above 600, take the Stage 1 test before you skip anything. If you score well, you lose an hour. If you do not, you have found your fastest route to more wins.
:::

## How the exercises work

The exercises are the heart of the book. Each one gives a position and a task, such as "White to move. Win material" or "Black to move. Is the pawn on e4 safe to take? Play the right move." Every exercise has one move-based answer, though sometimes a second move is listed as equally good.

Work through them like this:

1. **Set up the position** on a board or in an app, from the side of the player to move.
2. **Do not move any pieces.** Look, think, and calculate in your head, as you would in a game.
3. **Write down your answer**, including the main reply you expect from your opponent. Writing forces you to commit. "I was going to play that" does not count.
4. **Check the solution** in the Solutions section at the back of the book. Read the explanation even when you were right; it often contains the reason you were right, which is the part you need next time.
5. **Mark the result**: correct first time, correct with the hint, or wrong.

Some exercises have no tactic at all. The correct answer is a sound, quiet move, and the trap is a flashy capture that does not work. These are deliberate. In real games, most moves are not tactics, and you need to know when to stop looking for one.

### When to move on

A good guideline is about **80% first-try accuracy** on a chapter's exercise set, which means 9 of 11. If you score lower, re-read the sections that cover the exercises you missed, wait a day, and try those exercises again. Do not grind the same set ten times in one evening: once you remember the answers, the set no longer tests anything.

### Revisiting your misses

Every exercise you get wrong goes onto a review list. Revisit each one after about **1 day, 3 days, 7 days and 21 days**. On each visit, set up the position fresh and solve it again without looking at your old notes. If you solve it cleanly twice in a row, take it off the list. A simple notebook works fine: write the exercise number and the four dates when you first miss it.

:::nextgame Start your review list today
Open a notebook or a notes file and give it two sections: "Exercises to review" and "Mistakes from my games". You will use both in every chapter.
:::

## Using a board or a free app

You can use this book with nothing more than a chess set. That is the best way to read the chapters, because moving real pieces with your hands helps you keep track of the position. A screen is equally good for the exercises, and it is the easiest way to play out positions afterwards.

Every diagram and exercise in the book comes with a **FEN**, a single line of text that describes a position exactly. Chapter 2 explains how to read one. To load a FEN into a free app:

1. Open the analysis board of any free chess website or app.
2. Look for an option called "Set up position", "Load FEN", "Board editor" or similar.
3. Paste or type the FEN and confirm. Check that the correct side is to move.

Once a position is on the screen, you can do three useful things with it:

* **Solve first, then check.** Switch the engine off, solve the position, then switch the engine on to confirm your answer.
* **Play it out against a friend.** Set up the position on a real board, and take the side that should be winning. Your friend defends. If you cannot win a "winning" position against a human, you have found something to practise.
* **Play it out against an engine.** Many apps let you play from any position against a computer at a chosen strength. This is especially useful for the endgame chapters, where knowing the right idea is not enough until you can carry it out.

:::mistake Letting the engine think for you
The engine is a tool for checking your thinking, not a substitute for it. If you open the engine before you have decided on your own move, you will feel that you understand the position and remember very little. Decide first, then check.
:::

## Play slow games

You do not need to play blitz or bullet at all to use this book. In fact, for the next few months, they will mostly train you to repeat your current habits faster.

The book's routines (the safety scan, counting, looking for candidate moves) take time to run. At first they take a lot of time. You need games where you can afford that.

* **Recommended:** 15 minutes plus 10 seconds per move, or slower. Thirty-minute games are excellent.
* **Also excellent:** correspondence or "daily" chess, where you have a day or more per move. It is ideal for practising the full thinking routine without time pressure.
* **Over the board:** if you play at a club, longer time limits are usually standard, which suits this book perfectly.

Timed play is optional throughout. The rules about clocks are explained briefly in Chapter 1, and practical time management is covered in Chapter 27. Until then, give yourself enough time to think.

:::coach Fewer, better games
Coaching judgment: three slow games that you analyze properly are worth more than thirty fast games that you never look at again. If you love fast chess, play it for fun, but keep a separate slow-game rating that you use to measure progress.
:::

## Companion products

The book is complete on its own. Three optional companions use the same positions and the same methods:

* **The Cheat Sheets.** One printable page per chapter with the chapter's decision procedure, the same steps as the `cheat` box at the end of each chapter. Keep the current one next to your board while you play slow games, until the steps become automatic.
* **The 90-Day Accelerator workbook.** A day-by-day schedule that tells you which chapter to read, which exercises to solve, which games to play, and when to review your misses. Use it if you prefer to follow a plan rather than set your own pace.
* **The Interactive Trainer.** The book's exercises in an app that lets you play the moves on screen, checks your answer, shows the hint and explanation, and schedules your review dates for you.

None of these is required. Everything they contain can be done with the book, a notebook and a free app.

## What is evidence, and what is coaching judgment

Chess instruction is full of confident advice, and much of it has never been tested. This book tries to be honest about the difference.

When a recommendation is supported by published research, it appears in an **evidence** box with the source. When it is the accumulated experience of chess coaching, it appears in a **coach** box, or is described as a guideline. Both are useful. Only the first has been measured. The research below underpins the way the exercises and review schedule are designed.

:::evidence Testing yourself beats rereading
Roediger and Karpicke (2006), "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention," *Psychological Science*. Students who practised recalling material remembered more of it a week later than students who spent the same time rereading it. This is why the book asks you to solve exercises and write down answers, not just play through solutions.
:::

:::evidence Spacing your review works better than cramming
Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006), "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis," *Psychological Bulletin*. Across many studies, practice spread over time produced better long-term retention than the same amount of practice massed together. The 1-3-7-21 day review schedule applies this idea; the exact intervals are coaching judgment.
:::

:::evidence Serious study matters in chess
Charness, Tuffiash, Krampe, Reingold and Vasyukova (2005), "The role of deliberate practice in chess expertise," *Applied Cognitive Psychology*. Among tournament players, the amount of time spent in serious solitary study, such as analyzing positions and games, was strongly associated with chess skill. Playing alone was a weaker predictor. This is why the loop includes analysis and fixing, not only playing.
:::

:::evidence Strong players see patterns, not single pieces
Chase and Simon (1973), "Perception in chess," *Cognitive Psychology*. Stronger players reconstructed briefly shown game positions far better than weaker players, but the advantage largely disappeared with randomly placed pieces. Expertise depends on recognizing familiar groups of pieces, often called **chunks**. This is why the book repeats key patterns many times in realistic positions.
:::

Everything else in this book, including the rating milestones, the 80% guideline and the order of the chapters, is coaching judgment. It is built on what usually works for improving players, but treat it as advice, not law. If something clearly does not work for you, adjust it.

## A quick word on notation

The book uses standard algebraic notation, the same system every chess app and book uses. Every square has a name made of a letter (the file, a to h) and a number (the rank, 1 to 8): the square in the bottom-left corner from White's side is a1.

Moves are written with a capital letter for the piece and the square it moves to: K for king, Q for queen, R for rook, B for bishop and N for knight. Pawns have no letter. So `Nf3` means "a knight moves to f3", and `e4` means "a pawn moves to e4". An `x` means a capture (`Bxf7`), `+` means check, and `#` means checkmate. `O-O` is castling kingside and `O-O-O` is castling queenside.

When a move is numbered, `12.Nf3` is White's 12th move and `12...Nf6` is Black's reply. Throughout the book, **White** and **Black** with capital letters mean the players.

If notation is new to you, read [Chapter 2](#ch-02) before going further. It takes about an hour to learn, and after that every diagram and line in this book will be easy to follow.

## Your first week

To get started:

1. Read [Chapter 1](#ch-01) with a board in front of you, even if you think you know the rules. Most players discover at least one rule they had wrong.
2. Solve the Chapter 1 exercises and start your review list.
3. Read [Chapter 2](#ch-02) and practise writing down a short game.
4. Play two slow games and keep a scoresheet for each.
5. After each game, spend ten minutes finding the move where it turned, before using an engine.

Then turn the page and begin.
